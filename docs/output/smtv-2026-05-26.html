Crypto barely moved. $BTC held $77,000, $ETH sat at $2,120, and $SOL hovered near $85, with the total market cap parked around $2.55 trillion. None of that is the interesting part. The interesting part is everything happening around it. Stocks printed fresh records, bonds bounced, volatility stayed pinned, and a wave of speculative money poured into space stocks and a giant new IPO filing.

Every gauge of risk appetite ran hot. The one bucket that did not catch a bid was crypto. The froth is very real right now, and for once it is funneling somewhere other than alts. That is worth sitting with, because the speculative dollar usually finds crypto early. This week it found rockets.

The tape made new highs almost everywhere. The Dow rose half a percent, about 255 points, to a fresh all-time high, and the move was not just a megacap illusion. The equal-weighted Dow and the equal-weighted S&P 500 also closed at records, which means the average stock came along rather than a handful of giants dragging the index up. (Equal weight counts every member the same, so it reads breadth, the participation of the rank and file rather than the top five names.) The desk had been flagging the Dow's failure to make new highs as the one thing nagging at them. That nag cleared.

The Nasdaq 100 added more than a percent to around 29,900, with futures sitting 100 points shy of 30,000. Bonds, which had looked broken for weeks, bounced hard, with 30-year futures up a full percent. $TLT, the long-dated Treasury bond fund, held a price shelf that has stopped every selloff going back to the mid-2000s. Crude fell 4 percent to $93 even with the usual Strait of Hormuz headlines floating around. Gold and silver each slipped half a percent. Nvidia had reported earnings during the break, and the reaction was a nothing burger, a textbook pullback and nothing more.

Two things are worth filing away under the surface of all these highs. The ratio of consumer-discretionary stocks to consumer-staples has been rolling over, and that kind of defensive shift (money leaving retail and travel for groceries and household goods) has a habit of showing up before market peaks. The second is a portfolio note that is easy to miss. If you equal-weight a basket of sectors and technology is one of them, you are underweight the very thing carrying the index higher. When one sector does the lifting, equal weight is functionally underweight, and that is how a lot of cautious portfolios quietly fall behind a market that keeps ripping.

Chapter 3 · The SpaceX Filing

An AI Company Wearing a Rocket Files the Biggest Deal in a Decade

SpaceX filed to go public. The S-1 hit while the desk was traveling, so they came back to it, and the scale is hard to overstate. By their read the deal would hand early investors and employees more cash out than every IPO of the past decade put together. Barron's marked the moment with a cover, a rocket on the front and the words too big to buy. Every big, splashy IPO the desk can name got smoked after it listed, which is the case for fading this one. The trouble is that a too-big-to-buy magazine cover is exactly the kind of consensus that tends to be wrong, and by the end of the segment they had mostly talked themselves into buying it.

The filing carries a tell about what SpaceX actually is. Sort the company by where its money goes and it spends more like an AI company than a rocket company, because AI dwarfs every other line item in its expenses. That fits a pattern the desk keeps returning to, where the biggest companies all pour their cash into the same buildout, and the firms that already have the money are the ones positioned to stay on top.

The incentives are their own story. The filing lays out a package that would award Elon Musk roughly a billion SpaceX shares, but only if three things happen at once. The company has to reach a $7.5 trillion valuation, SpaceX has to put a permanent colony of at least one million people on Mars, and Musk has to still be employed when both clear. It sounds absurd, and the desk remembered that everyone laughed at the Tesla pay package as unhittable too, right up until he hit it.

There is a mechanical wrinkle beyond the deal itself. SpaceX is set to be expedited into the major stock indexes, which forces every index fund to buy it and can scramble the big benchmarks for weeks. The desk plans to track that noise through June.

Underneath the headline, the whole space complex is on fire. The space-sector ETF, a basket fund of aerospace and exploration names, has been gapping up most mornings and trading like a meme stock. $PL (Planet Labs, satellite imaging) broke out of a multi-year base to new highs. $RKLB (Rocket Lab, small-satellite launch) keeps running. $ASTS (AST SpaceMobile, satellite-to-phone service) is coiled tight, $LUNR (Intuitive Machines, lunar landers) is climbing out of a base, and $BKSY (BlackSky) and $SATL (Satellogic) are along for the ride. In the desk's words it is the single best-looking thematic in the market.

The crypto angle

The impulse driving a meme-stock bid into space names and a too-big-to-buy IPO is the same speculative impulse that used to rip through alts on any given week. Right now it is finding a home in equities instead. When that risk appetite eventually rotates, the open question for crypto is whether it gets a turn or whether the speculative dollar has simply found newer toys.
