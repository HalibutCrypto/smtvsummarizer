Bitcoin gave back roughly one and a half percent overnight to $64,850. Ethereum dropped a hair under two percent to $1,895. $SOL slid to $76.50. Aggregate crypto cap eased to $2.19T. There is no crypto-specific story on the tape this morning and Louis is not on. The Friday desk is where crypto gets its own hour.

The macro handed crypto every bearish card in the deck overnight. The dollar broke out of a multi-month base, oil ripped to $90 on a +4.25% overnight session, the 10-year yield sat near a 52-week high, and silver got cut three percent. That is the classic risk-off intermarket pattern for anything speculative, and crypto sat inside it quietly. The complex went along for the ride rather than fought it.

Where crypto brushed the show was through the Big Tech capex discourse. Alphabet posting a negative free-cash-flow quarter for the first time in its history matters for anyone tracking the AI and data-center trade that miners have been riding. If the cash cows spending on data centers just started burning cash faster than they generate it, the second-order compute-buildout thesis that $WGMI and the miner group have been sold on gets a harder time.

Chapter 2 · Lead Story

Alphabet Just Ran Out of Free Cash Flow for the First Time in Its History

$GOOGL reported after the close last night. Revenue and operating income were records. That was not the story. The story was the cash-flow statement. For the first time in the company's history, Alphabet posted a quarter of negative free cash flow. Operating cash could not cover the AI capex. Stock down big pre-market. There was a small boost from an Alphabet investment in SpaceX that flowed through the print, and even that footnote could not save the reaction.

The concern is not this quarter's headline number. The concern is what negative free cash flow does to the risk tolerance of a business that had been building the next decade's compute on the assumption its printing press would keep printing. Mag Seven ex-Tesla built its identity as a cash-flow monster, with the money made every quarter reinvested into whatever came next. That structural cushion is what let these companies chase moonshots for a decade. Once operating cash cannot cover investing cash, the margin for error on any single AI bet gets thinner. Miss the AI wave, the stock breaks. Cut capex to preserve cash flow, the stock breaks. There is no clean way out from here.

$TSLA is a different story with the same fingerprint. Down about seven percent pre-market. Regulatory-credit revenue collapsed. Automotive margins compressed. Full self-driving now has 1.5 million paid subscriptions at $100 a month, 2.5 million paid robotaxi miles logged in the quarter, and about ten percent weekly growth in US robotaxi presence. The stock does not care about the car business anymore. It cares about whether the market still believes 90% of cars on the road in five to ten years are Teslas. That is what Tesla actually trades on. Every quarter the stock gets tested on whether that belief is intact. Today the belief held. The car economics did not. And the stock still got hit because the correlation between $TSLA and SpaceX has fully re-coupled for July, so Tesla trades every day now with a name whose freefall has no obvious stopping point.

Service Now printed after the bell. Software had sold off ahead of the report yesterday morning and then reversed hard into it, with $NOW itself trading up in the pre-market this morning. If it lands a strong green session, the software group tends to follow, since $NOW reports first among the software heavies and sets the tone for $CRM, $MSFT, and $INTU. $IBM pre-announced a bad quarter a week ago and still got sold anyway when the print landed. Peacock, Comcast's streaming service, posted its first quarterly profit. Defense primes Lockheed and Raytheon lifted on the Golden Dome theme. $CSX beat again, extending a multi-quarter railroad run. $T (AT&T) opened up about four and a half percent, a move it almost never makes in either direction on any given day.

Chapter 3 · Breadth

Pipelines, Malls, Cigarettes, and Fashion Are All at New Highs and the Discourse Skips Every One

The tape's leadership is much broader than the tickers people fight about on social media. Multi-year highs this morning showed up in $PAA (Plains All American Pipeline, midstream oil pipelines), $PK (Park Hotels & Resorts, upscale hotel REIT spun out of Hilton), $CTVA (Corteva, agricultural inputs including seed genetics and crop-protection chemistry), and $PM (Philip Morris International, tobacco including the Marlboro international franchise and the Zyn nicotine-pouch brand). Pipelines, hotels, seeds, cigarettes. Four sectors with nothing structurally in common except that all four charts printed the same kind of breakout at the same time.

The consumer side of the tape is doing the same thing, and the online discourse keeps missing it. $SPG (Simon Property Group, the largest US shopping-mall REIT) is at an all-time high. The mall has been called dead every year since roughly 2015, and yet the largest mall landlord in the country now trades at a level that never existed before. $VSCO (Victoria's Secret, the lingerie and beauty retailer spun out of L Brands in 2021) is at an all-time high after breaking out of a multi-month base and holding the earnings gap that came with it. $IMAX beat pre-Odyssey release and continues to shape up as a buyout target. $MODG (Callaway Golf) is working. $AAPL printed a fresh all-time high last week, and almost nothing was written about that either. The Mag Seven has not led the broader market since October or November of last year, a nine-month stretch during which the tape kept making new highs on the backs of names nobody was tracking.

The framework the desk kept coming back to is simple. If you don't follow what everyone talks about on social media, there is a lot of leadership happening across the globe. Pipelines, malls, tobacco, and agricultural chemistry do not make for good bull-market memes. They make for good returns. The rally has been broader than the discourse for the better part of a year, and the last two earnings mornings from Big Tech are what it takes for the rest of the market to finally catch a glance.
