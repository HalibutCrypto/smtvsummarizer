Spot barely moved on the open. $BTC sat at $80,240 +0.3%, $ETH at $2,300 flat, $SOL at $88.50 +0.5%. Total crypto market cap printed $2.64T, the highest close since January. The story under the prices is more interesting than the prices. [3:55]

Louis Sykes flagged the dislocation. The Bitcoin breakout has been orderly. No vertical move, no euphoria candle, just a steady grind off recent lows. Speculators in the perpetual markets, the cryptonative futures, are leaning the other way. Funding rates are negative, which is a clean tell that traders are paying to be short into the breakout. When price does one thing and positioning does another, that gap is where squeezes live. October 10 of last year was the textbook example in the opposite direction (long crowd over-leveraged into weakness, then got steamrolled). The setup here is the mirror. Louis sees $BTC at $100K in the next few months. [20:30]

The miner trade has gone full data center. $HUT, $WULF, and $APLD have all transitioned away from pure-play Bitcoin mining into compute infrastructure stocks. The moves are vertical. Profit-taking is reasonable here, though the broader disbelief in the rally is still high enough that the trade is probably not finished. [22:30]

Louis spent most of his airtime on the framework underneath crypto. The standard pitch on crypto is decentralization. He thinks that frame is wrong. Tether stopping service to Venezuelan users a few weeks ago made the point cleanly. If the issuer is centralized, the network does not save you. The real prize is composability. Brokers, the New York Stock Exchange, the DTCC, the NSCC, the Federal Reserve, and individual banks each run their own internal databases. Reconciling those ledgers takes days, opens a settlement window where counterparty risk forces brokers to post collateral, and supports an army of six-figure Wall Street jobs whose entire role is checking that one spreadsheet matches another. Louis called it a hidden tax on humanity. The asset managers building the new rails reportedly think the eventual impact on the global economy will be larger than artificial intelligence. [16:50]

Chapter 3 · The Late-Cycle Clock

The IPO Wave Has Three Historical Comparables. All Three Ended Badly.

SpaceX and Anthropic are queuing up the largest equity supply event in a century.

The biggest IPO wave in modern memory is queued. SpaceX is reportedly talking $1.5T to $2T. Anthropic is in the same conversation. Exchanges are reportedly changing rules so the largest of these can enter the major indices on day one. The usual S&P committee timeline gets bypassed. Public buyers will get index-fund-driven demand from minute one of trading. [44:05]

The historical comp set is short. Three events in 124 years.

1901

US Steel formed

JP Morgan rolled Carnegie Steel and smaller mills into a single entity worth roughly 15% of the entire stock market. Dow peaked weeks later and got slaughtered.

1919

RCA consolidation

Asset rollup, functionally identical in float impact. Two weeks later the Dow peaked and got cut in half.

1956

Ford IPO

Worth roughly 1% of US GDP at debut. The Ford family was diversifying their portfolio rather than raising capital. Market peaked in April. SpaceX is running the same playbook now.

The other complication this cycle is that companies stayed private far longer than they used to. Valuations are stretched at the print. If the IPO window lands at the wrong moment, the public buyer is the bag. The desk is not panicking yet. The trade stays long until the supply actually hits, then the comp-set framework gets a real test. [45:00]

Global degeneracy check

Eric Balchunas flagged this morning that a 2x leveraged SK Hynix ETF is now the fourth-largest fund on the Hong Kong exchange by AUM. The US-equivalent scale is roughly a $750B single-stock leveraged fund. Korean and Hong Kong retail are running ahead of the US on degeneracy this cycle. That demand is part of what the IPO machine is going to monetize. [1:06:55]
