Louis Sykes joined from a New Zealand Airbnb at one in the morning with a spotty connection, and Bitcoin sat pinned at $65,000, the level Spencer called the “scene of the crime.” Louis's price read was one sentence. Above sixty, he likes it. The actual meat of the Friday hour lived somewhere else entirely.

Broadridge Financial ran an institutional survey on tokenization timelines this week and the numbers collapsed the range people in the space had been using. Money-market funds materially tokenize in four years by the median institutional estimate. Louis's own prior guess was late 2030s, closer to 2040. Two independent sources he talked to this week (Chris Perkins at Franklin Templeton and a seventy-year-old ETF veteran who started as a paper-record runner in the 1960s) both told him the same thing about the mechanism. Tokenization is being built as the next wrapper for the existing ETF market. A token can hold the fund itself, which means it trades around the clock, and it can be programmed. If a client wants the S&P 500 with three specific stocks stripped out, an ETF cannot deliver that. A tokenized fund can. Louis flagged the combination as “completely just not being talked about.”

The end of the Wild West arrived on the same day. BitMEX is shutting down. Arthur Hayes invented the perpetual swap at BitMEX in 2016, and the insurance fund the exchange built out of liquidation fees quietly compounded into one of the best-performing pools of Bitcoin anywhere, roughly four billion dollars from a base near zero. That book is closing. The institutions absorbing the technology into the mainstream are the DTCC (rolling out its own tokenization service), $JPM and Citigroup (both building tokenized deposits scheduled for full operational launch in the second half of next year), and $BK, Bank of New York Mellon, the world's largest custodian, now printing all-time highs as the market prices it as the biggest single institutional beneficiary of the trend. Louis's forecast for the next few years is an acquisition spree, with traditional banks buying up the private companies quietly building the tokenization stack.

Jackson Hole ties the ribbon. Every August the Kansas City Fed hosts a symposium that is usually forgettable monetary-policy talk. 1985 was the exception, with the high-and-rising-dollar theme that led into the Plaza Accord to devalue the dollar a few weeks later. 2007 was the other one, with housing on the agenda months before the housing market cracked. This year's theme is financial technology, innovation, payments, and stable coins. Louis said it is the first time tokenization has been on the Fed's radar in that room. Kevin Walsh, in the running for the next Fed chair on some short lists, personally holds nearly two hundred million dollars in digital assets, including live positions in DeFi lending and decentralized exchanges. Stable coins are legally required to hold US Treasury bills as collateral, and even at today's early stage they already rank as the seventh or eighth largest holder of US Treasuries globally.

Chapter 2 · Markets

The Dollar Breakout the DXY Prints and the Equal-Weighted G10 Basket Doesn't

The show's title was the dollar. $DXY (the ICE US Dollar Index, weighted heaviest to the euro and yen) looks like it broke out of a multi-month flag on the front chart, right at the upper bound of the base. Strazza's caveat is the entire reason to pay attention. An equal-weighted basket of the developed G10 currencies is still range-bound, and emerging currencies are actually stronger than the developed set. The DXY breakout is a yen-and-euro weakness story dressed up as broad dollar strength, since those two currencies dominate the index weighting. Confirmation would need the equal-weighted G10 basket to break higher too. Until then, the chart says one thing about the dollar and the currencies underneath the chart say another.

JC pushed back on the immediate risk-asset implication. The dollar has been printing higher highs and higher lows all year and stocks have done fine, which he read as impressive resilience. Strazza responded with the timing. Since the dollar broke out of its base in the second week of June, $NDX, $SPX, and big-cap tech growth all stalled around June 10 and have been messy and sideways since. Yesterday's rotation belongs to the same picture. Financials, industrials, healthcare, energy, and utilities were up. Everything else was heavy. Google and Tesla got smacked after earnings, Intel reports today, and tech and big-cap growth are trading as the funding source for the rotation into the boring cyclicals.

The other side of the same rotation is where the highs are being printed. $JPM made another all-time high yesterday alongside Goldman, Morgan Stanley, and Bank of America. Jamie Dimon showed up in headlines earlier this week telling people not to buy stocks. He did not actually say that. What he said was that he does not like buying bonds and that stocks with good valuations are worth buying, and the headline was a fugazi. The desk noted his June 2022 hurricane-and-recession call for perspective. Since Dimon made that call, $JPM shares have literally doubled the return of the $SPX. His job is to grow shareholder value at JP Morgan, and he is doing it.

Chapter 3 · Sentiment

AAII Bulls Just Hit a Ten-Month Low Two and a Half Percent From a New All-Time High

JC pulled up the American Association of Individual Investors survey and had to download the raw data to find the last time bulls were this rare. The answer was September of last year. Bulls have not been this scarce in ten months, and bears just spiked to their highest reading over the same window. The $SPX sits about two and a half percent from a fresh all-time high while this reading was landing.

The context is what makes the print odd. JC checked 1987, 2000, and 2007. In every one of those cases AAII was bullish right before the top, with retail long into the peak. This week the peak is directly in front of the tape and retail has moved the other direction. Financials and industrials are running. $JPM and the big-bank group are at all-time highs. The advance-decline line and equal-weighted breadth are healthy. The rotation is doing what rotations are supposed to do. Nothing in the market itself is warning of an event, and the sentiment survey is warning of one anyway.

The honest read from the desk was that nobody on the panel has a clean explanation. JC's summary was that it is hard to imagine what people are so scared about. The disconnect itself is what to flag. Historically it resolves by the sentiment catching up to the price, and bulls came back before the last several moves higher. If that pattern repeats, the tape has more room in it than the mood suggests.
