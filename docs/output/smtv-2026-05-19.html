The cleanest crypto framework on the show came from Strazza. Drop an anchored VWAP from the February 6 intraday low and run it across the big names. $ETH has been consolidating right on that line since roughly 6pm Sunday. $SOL has been doing the same since Sunday night. That is the level where the short-and-intermediate-term trend lives. A clean hold and the bid is intact. A clean break and there is nothing to lean on until much lower.

$BTC is the cleanest of the three. It is nowhere near the February 6 VWAP. It is retesting the mid-March high that was its base breakout level on the way up in April. The April retest already held once. This is a second look at the same shelf, and it qualifies as the best-looking chart of the major crypto trio.

Underneath that, $HYPE is breakaway gapping +10% premarket to fresh all-time highs. The desk gave Louis the credit for it. He has been pounding the table on Hyperliquid for a while and JC has had the trade on the back of his pick. The use-case angle matters here. JC noted that he actually uses the product, particularly to watch S&P futures on weekends, and is rarely in a position to make money on something he genuinely uses.

The cleanest bearish tell in the data is in ETH positioning. Commercial hedgers, the ones who tend to be right, are dumping. Speculators are loaded. That kind of split has historically been the wrong combination for spot. Until the speculators flush, ETH will keep struggling to clear the same ceiling it has spent the last several weeks under.

Three months and counting since the Dow Jones Industrial Average put in a new high. Every other major US index has cleared its February print, in some cases by a lot. Strazza put up best-April-ever and record-month numbers across the desk’s books over the same window. The Dow, by itself, has gone nowhere.

The mechanical reason is the weighting. The Dow is price-weighted, which means a $400 stock matters more than a $200 stock regardless of company size. In a 2026 tape where the biggest companies in the world trade at huge market caps but split-adjusted lower share prices, that quirk has consequences. UnitedHealth carries the most weight in the index. Goldman and Caterpillar are next. Microsoft is the largest tech holding at 5%. Sherwin Williams ($SHW), a paint company, carries more index weight than Apple, Amazon, or Nvidia. The three megacaps that drive the rest of the tape barely move the needle here.

Spencer’s read is that the Dow still matters as a rule. When the index that has run for a century stops printing highs, history says you respect it, even if the math behind the index is dated. Strazza pushed back. He thinks the price-weighted construction has lost most of its information content for a tape now driven by the megacaps that barely register here. The YouTube chat split 53-47 in favor of caring. As tight a poll as the show has run.

The other live thread on the desk was Nike ($NKE). It is the next assumed deletion, and the desk treated the replacement as an open game. Spencer wants Exxon ($XOM) brought back, the move they made with GE years ago. JC and Strazza leaned Google. Tesla, Meta, and Eli Lilly would all need to split first to fit the price-weighted format. The committee tends to chase recognizable household names, which is why Booking, ASML-equivalents, and pure infrastructure plays never make the cut.

Underneath the Dow story, the broader tape was soft. KOSPI down 3.2% overnight kept the global-risk narrative front and center. Crude held above $103 with copper and silver giving back a percent each. Gold sat flat at $4,550. The 30-year bond future was unchanged near 110.12, a pause after weeks of drama in fixed income. And the US dollar continued to grind lower off the 2018-era ceiling that capped it. The piece that comes with that is international equity exposure has stopped acting like dead weight on US-resident portfolios for the first time in roughly a decade. The desk is using broad EFA and EM exposure as the top-down starting point and then drilling into countries only after the trend confirms itself. ASML is the cleanest single-stock proxy if you want one name.

The crypto angle

A breaking dollar and bid commodities are the macro setup BTC normally responds to. Gold is acting fine. The fact that $BTC is sitting at $76,850 flat into the same backdrop is the gap to watch. The macro is constructive. The participation has not arrived yet. Same story as yesterday and the day before, with the small difference that the dollar move is now visible on a chart that has been quiet for years.

Chapter 3 · Framework

Nvidia’s Own Equity Portfolio Has Been a Quiet Stock-Picking Edge

Spencer spent part of the show on Nvidia’s 13F. The filing is public and free to read. What sits inside it is a list of equity stakes Nvidia’s own treasury team has accumulated in companies that are mostly its customers, suppliers, or strategic partners. The aggregate position runs roughly $30 billion. That is bigger than most hedge funds. As a stock-picking signal, it has been working.

Intel ($INTC) is the largest holding. Nvidia bought it last year and it has roughly doubled since. They own about 4% of the company. CoreWeave ($CRWV), the AI cloud-compute name, was a pre-IPO position they added to on the IPO and have been adding to since. It is currently sitting at the anchored VWAP from its all-time highs, which the desk treats as a real test. Nokia ($NOK) is in the book and roughly tripled after they bought it. Coherent ($COHR), the optical-components name, is at fresh all-time highs. Synopsys ($SNPS), the chip-design software company, is also in there. So is Soundhound ($SOUN).

The legal question came up on the show. Nvidia is a regulated insider on several of these. The line is that equity investments are fine. They hold strategic stakes for the long term. Day-trading ahead of guidance would be a different category and would be a problem. Buying CoreWeave equity and sitting on it for years is none of that. Strazza framed Nvidia as the most transparent window the public has into who is actually consuming AI compute right now, because the company is in the room with all of them before the rest of the market gets the data.

The same conversation produced a useful rule for IPOs in general. Two anchored levels matter post-listing. The anchored VWAP from the IPO day, and the offering price itself. Above both, it is hard to be bearish. Below both, it is hard to be bullish. The reference data is bleak. Roughly 90% of IPOs trade below their offering price at some point. After Blackstone went public in 2007 and immediately cratered 90%, the post-financial-crisis era has been dominated by IPOs that drew down 50% before they ever doubled. Alibaba, Meta, Uber, Coinbase. The desk reads CoreWeave through that lens. The retest at the anchored VWAP from the high is exactly where most post-2007 IPOs have either confirmed or failed.
