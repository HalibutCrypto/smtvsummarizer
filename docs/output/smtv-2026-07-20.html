A treasury company at that size is now a real overhang on any ether unwind, since a stack that big has to sell into something.

Personalis ($PCNL, precision genomics) getting acquired by Tempus ($TEM), though the premium under $16 a share is thin enough to sting existing holders.

If the platform lets you have as many shares as you want at an IPO, you probably do not want them, because the reason the shares are available is that the insiders are trying to unload.

Chapter 3 · Framework

The Bears' Favorite Chart Is Using the Wrong Denominator

The chart that goes viral every few months, and did again this weekend, is US margin debt plotted against US GDP. It shows a line running to record highs and gets shared with the argument that the stock market is dangerously leveraged and about to snap. JC's take, which took most of ten minutes of the show, was that the chart is drawn wrong. Margin debt is money that traders borrow against their stock holdings to buy more stock. GDP is the country's total output of goods and services. There is no direct link between the two. The correct denominator is the market cap of the stock market itself, because that is what margin debt is a claim on.

When you swap denominators, the picture inverts. Margin debt as a percent of US stock market cap sits well below the readings that preceded the 1987 crash and the 2008 financial crisis. It is genuinely low for this stage of a bull cycle. The puzzle is not "why are people so leveraged," it is "why is not more margin being used given how far the tape has run." Two-thirds of adult Americans still believe a recession is imminent, and that positioning survey shows up in the margin data as underexposure, which inverts the usual scare-chart framing.

The natural rebuttal is that retail moved from cash margin into leveraged ETFs, so the traditional gauge misses the real leverage. Add every inverse and leveraged ETF on the US tape together and the combined AUM is under $200B. That is roughly a quarter of one percent of US stock market cap. A rounding error, even before adjusting for the fact that these products are designed to be day-traded and rarely held for more than a session. Two things can both be true. The category is growing fast, and it is still tiny in absolute size.

None of this means bears are wrong that the market can correct. It means the specific bear case that circulates every weekend on financial television, retail leveraged to the eyeballs and about to blow up, is not what the data shows. Someone who wants to be short needs a better reason. The desk closed the segment with the reminder that they are welcome to make the trade, but they should bring a better chart.

The reason to notice

The audience for the wrong-denominator chart is not traders. It is retirees and financial advisers who watch cable and read newspapers, and every one of them who moves to cash on a bad chart pushes their real return down for the next cycle. This is why the debunk mattered enough for the show to spend ten minutes on it.
