IBM pre-announced. The next scheduled print was a week out. Late in the quarter, management watched a wave of enterprise clients shift capex out of software and into AI servers, memory, and storage, so they got out ahead of the report with preliminary numbers below estimates. The reaction was a twenty-two percent open-lower. The only two comparable IBM sessions in the last quarter century are a pair of fifteen-percent drops during the 2000-2001 dotcom unwind. The last time IBM traded down more than twenty percent in a session was October 19th, 1987. Black Monday.

Zoom out on the chart and it's less dramatic than the tape. The stock is still inside the same range it has traded since Q1 2025. The 2013 highs are what has to hold. Strazza's read was that this now looks distributive inside that two-year range. A breakdown still requires a clean loss of the support at those 2013 highs. Until that happens, the tape is ugly and the chart is intact.

The sympathy tape was worse than IBM itself in percentage terms. Workday down ten, Atlassian down nine, HubSpot down eight, ServiceNow and Accenture both down seven and a half. The wider frame is that software and semis are still moving in opposite directions week over week. Money is not leaving tech on days like this. It's rotating from software into servers, memory, and networking inside the same enterprise budgets.

Bank earnings came in clean across the majors. JPMorgan, Bank of America, and Wells Fargo all posted the same broad picture. Loan growth healthy, trading revenue solid, deal revenue recovering, consumer credit stable, delinquencies low. Jamie Dimon's release language leaned harder on the tailwinds than usual, calling out AI-driven capital investment, fiscal stimulus, Golden Dome, and, most importantly, the benefits of more efficient regulation. That last one is the one to underline. Fifteen years of post-crisis capital constraints on the biggest banks are being rolled back, and Wells Fargo in particular has spent most of that period under a growth cap that's now getting eased. Morgan Stanley, BlackRock, PNC, and Bank of New York Mellon report tomorrow. Regionals hit Wednesday and Thursday.

CPI came in below on both prints. Headline 3.5% year-over-year against a 3.8% estimate. Core 2.6% versus 2.9%. Thirty-year bond futures caught a bid, rates leaked lower, and the metals complex went straight up. Gold added two and a third percent pushing $4,100. Silver added two and a half pushing $60. Copper up two, crude up three near $81. The Dow was down 600 into the pre-market on the IBM print, then flat on the CPI. VIX sits under 17.

One positioning setup to keep on the desk

Speculators have never been more short yen in the history of the futures data. Japanese rates have moved from negative to over two percent in a straight line. Dollar-yen is sitting right into a 1.27 long-term price projection off the Bretton Woods low. The Nikkei just tagged a 1.618 projection from its 1970s low, and $EWJ (the dollar-priced Japan ETF) shows a bearish divergence at resistance. Historical parallel is the tiny 2024 BOJ move that unwound the carry trade globally in a session. If the Bank of Japan so much as speaks this week, that positioning gets its face ripped off, and the whole risk complex moves with it.

Chapter 3 · The Framework

The Bank Index Cleared Its January 2007 High for the First Time in Nineteen Years

JC has been calling the S&P bank index “the most important chart in the world” going into this week's earnings. The specific index he means is the equal-weight one, roughly ninety bank stocks ($KBE is the retail proxy). Equal weighting matters here. It gives you a clean read on the banking industry as a group rather than a JPMorgan-plus-friends aggregate weighted by the top five.

The January 2007 high on that index was the last major top before financials led the market into the great financial crisis. Financials peaked nine months before the S&P did. That level has now been the ceiling for nineteen years. The COVID reclaim attempt failed. The 2024 attempt failed. The 2025 attempt failed. This week it cleared.

“This is brand new. This just happened. This isn't like an old thing. This is brand spanking new. So if you're bullish, you're betting on rising stocks, this confirms you're probably on the right track. However, if you are bearish of equities, if the bank index is above the 07 highs, you're wrong.”
JC, on the equal-weight bank index breakout [18:42]

Strazza's read agreed on the technical setup. The level was tested a handful of times before this print, and each attempt absorbed more of the overhead supply, which is what a real breakout wants to look like. Breadth across the financial sector is broad-based across the whole group. The Kenny-style caveat about how “last time we were here look what happened” got answered directly. Every prior visit to this level rolled over. This is the first time in modern history the index has cleared it.

The trade expression is the harder question. The big six banks ($JPM, $GS, $MS, $WFC, $BAC, $C) are already at or right below all-time highs. Chasing them is a bad entry. $KBE versus the S&P 500 has been sideways because tech's two-month run flattens every ratio chart against SPY. Even $KBE against the Dow is still below its November 2024 highs. JC's answer was that the bank index breakout isn't a signal to buy the banks specifically. It's a signal to buy stocks, period. Financials are the confirming chart. The trade lives everywhere else in the market.

The historical parallel the desk kept returning to is Louise Yamada's line, repeated across the show, that the bigger the collapse, the longer the time needed for repair. The GFC took roughly nineteen years to grind through this specific level. Not eighteen months. Nineteen years. That's the frame worth carrying into the next few weeks of tape.

The Bottom Line

  1. The bear thesis now has to explain the bank chart. The equal-weight S&P bank index is above its January 2007 high on a daily close, for the first time since the great financial crisis. That level held as a ceiling through the COVID reclaim, the 2024 attempt, and the 2025 attempt. Losing it back on a weekly close reopens the question. Until then, JC's frame stands, and it's a hard one to argue against.
  2. The IBM print is a software-wide signal, not a market-wide one. $IBM cracked -22% because enterprise clients diverted capex toward AI servers, memory, and storage. The sympathy list ($WDAY, $TEAM, $HUBS, $NOW, $ACN) confirms the substitution is happening across the customer base. Software is losing budget to semis inside the same corporate wallet. That's the trade to size around, not the index level.
  3. Yen positioning is the tail risk sitting on top of the tape. Speculators have never been more short yen in the futures data. The Nikkei tagged a 1.618 long-term projection from the 1970s low. If the Bank of Japan intervenes or even signals this week, the unwind moves the whole risk complex, exactly the way the smaller 2024 BOJ move did. Sizing decisions this week should account for a right-tail yen move the same way they account for one in oil.
