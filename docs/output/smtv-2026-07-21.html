Overnight moved. The Kospi added three and a half percent, the Nikkei added three, and the yen cracked to its weakest levels in decades. Silver ran four percent, copper three, crude two, gold one. The US pre-market took the cue politely, with $SPX up a third of a percent at 7,511, NASDAQ 100 futures leading up more than one percent, and Dow futures adding a hundred at 52,170. The VIX stayed pinned at 18.

Under the polite index, the whipsaw ran again. Semis ripped, software sold, for the second straight session. Salesforce ($CRM) down three percent, ServiceNow ($NOW) down three, the whole software cohort giving back to a semiconductor tape that added double-digit-single-name moves. These are all tech stocks going in opposite directions on a daily basis, and JC's read is that this is not one investor swinging back and forth. It is massive positioning in favor of semis over software that got stretched over months and is now mean-reverting, and the reversion is volatile rather than smooth. Under all of that, on an equal-weight relative basis, software has been quietly gaining on semis for several months and just printed fresh three-month highs on the ratio.

The tape has bucked every seasonal weak spot in 2026, and the trend that got you here does not care what the calendar says next.

Chapter 3 · Framework

Bonds Moved to the Stocks Side of the Ledger and Energy Moved to the Other

The chart JC spent the segment on came from Todd Sohn, and it plots the rolling correlation of the S&P 500 against two other things at the same time. In blue, the S&P versus US energy stocks. In red, the S&P versus the US Treasury bond market. The blue line used to sit at the top of the chart, meaning energy tended to move with stocks. The red line used to sit at the bottom, meaning bonds tended to move against them. Every retirement portfolio in the country was designed around that geometry.

Somewhere in the last several years, the lines crossed. Bonds now move with stocks. Energy now moves against them. The famous sixty-forty portfolio, sixty percent stocks and forty percent bonds, was built on the assumption that when the stock side falls the bond side rises to soften the drawdown. That relationship broke in 2022, when stocks fell and bonds fell harder. JC said on air he had never seen that in his career. The relationship is stronger today than it was then.

The corollary is that the modern version of the sixty-forty must include commodities or energy. Energy is three percent of the S&P 500 today. It is zero percent of the NASDAQ 100. Investors do not own it because the last two decades of index math argued they did not need to. Now the correlation math argues they do, and the amount of institutional money still positioned like the old chart, across pension funds, financial-adviser books, and generic balanced strategies, is enormous. That is a multi-year rebalance the market has barely started to price.

Where it shows up first

Refiners are the leading edge inside the group. Crack spreads (the profit margin between what a refiner pays for crude and what it charges for the refined products it sells) have been ripping, and refiner stocks already had their leg higher and have led the sector for years. The desk's read is that this is not a beta trade on the price of oil. It is an alpha trade on a category the market is structurally underweight, and it plays out over years rather than weeks.
