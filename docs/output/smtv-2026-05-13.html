Crypto stayed quiet for a third straight session. $BTC sits at $80,000 down five hundred dollars on the day, $ETH is flat near $2,780, and $SOL slipped about two percent to $92.50. Total crypto market cap rounds to $2.64T. The desk has been adding to crypto positions in the SMTV portfolio over the past several days and Spencer noted that the recent trade tape across the team has gotten more aggressive on the long side.

The structural note today was the derivative sprawl forming on the ETH stack. The Bitmine treasury vehicle (the ETH-balance-sheet company that Tom Lee fronts) now has a leveraged ETF wrapped around it, plus listed options on top. Traders who want ETH exposure can pick from spot, spot ETFs, the Bitmine treasury company, the leveraged version of that company, or short-dated calls on the leveraged version. JC kept it short. "I am just a big fan of the underlying. Call me old-fashioned." [12:41] The pattern that built around crypto in this cycle mirrors the one that built around tech, layer on layer of derivative wrappers all riding the same handful of underlyings.

The macro backdrop is the more interesting line to pull on. PPI ran hot this morning. Copper printed an all-time high. Yields are creeping. The dollar is quiet. That mix is exactly the regime BTC has historically liked, even if the price has not yet decided to do anything about it.

The desk framed sector rotation as the lifeblood of any bull market and then laid out where the case for it actually sits today. The S&P 500 hangs flat at 7,420, the Nasdaq 100 ticks higher to 29,240, Dow futures slip on a couple of large components, and the VIX is awkwardly bid at 18 into an otherwise sleepy tape. The interesting story is underneath the surface. The industrials sector ETF $XLI, the financials sector ETF $XLF, and the communication services ETF $XLC are all building continuation patterns above flat 50-day moving averages. The chart read on XLI and XLF specifically is a textbook cup-and-handle. XLC has traced out an inverse head-and-shoulders. None of these are confirmed breakouts yet. They are setups, and they sit exactly where breadth would need to fire if the semiconductor parabola pauses for any extended stretch.

The financials thread carried the day's biggest argument. The S&P 500 financials index just closed at all-time lows relative to the S&P 500 itself, the worst reading in two decades of ratio history. The desk has been split on whether that signals weakness or simply reflects the index's tech weight. Today JC took the second view firmly. His frame is a denominator fallacy. On absolute price, XLF is not making lows. Small-cap and mid-cap financials are strong. The S&P is so heavy on technology weight that nearly every sector ratio against it looks broken, while the underlying prices in those sectors are doing just fine. To put money behind the argument, JC bought $BRK.B, the largest single component of the XLF, and layered short puts on top. Berkshire is the rotation horse he wants to ride into a broadening tape.

The other tell came from the speculative end of the spectrum. Goldman's basket of non-profitable tech, which acts as a clean proxy for risk appetite, has ripped over the past five or six weeks. The same crowd of growth names that were getting buried in February are now leading. Strazza framed it as real evidence the appetite has shifted under the surface, and the move has continuity to it rather than reading as a one-off bounce in losers. The desk also flagged that even in the slow-tape March-into-April stretch, semiconductors and hardware stayed strong while software was the soft spot. Software has since caught up, which is the cleanest version of breadth widening from the top of the tape.

Chapter 3 · The Bond Tape

The Bond Market Is Acting Funny and Most Portfolios Aren't Set Up for It

The other thread that ran through the show is what JC has been calling the bond tsunami. PPI came in hotter than expected this morning. The 10-year sits at 4.47%, the 30-year is bid, and inflation-protected treasuries (TIPs, which adjust principal to CPI) have been outpacing nominal yielding treasuries for months. TIPs outperformance is the bond market's way of telegraphing that inflation is going to surprise to the upside before the official prints catch up, and that is the script that has just played out.

JC's framing of why this matters runs through what most investors own without realizing it. Americans, and most non-Americans for that matter, are sitting in equity-heavy portfolios with little energy exposure and no commodity exposure. That posture is mathematically equivalent to being long bonds. If rates rip and commodities continue to rip with them, every equity asset that depends on a low discount rate gets repriced lower, and portfolios with no inflation hedges take the hit on both sides. The position is unintended. The exposure is real.

The volatility compression in treasuries adds to the case. Nothing has happened in three years. The 10-year yield is net flat since October 2022, even as the Federal Reserve started cutting, and the long end has actually marched higher since the first cut. Vol compression that lasts that long always resolves one direction or the other. The desk's quiet view is that the resolution is more likely up than down, and the early 2000s rhyme is the historical reference they keep coming back to. When yields fell into the early 2000s, energy stocks went on one of their best runs ever. The relationship between rates, equities, and commodities is regime-dependent, and the regime may be flipping right under the tape.

The Signals Stacking Up

Copper at $6.62, fresh all-time high. Live cattle at all-time highs. Wheat, corn, and soybeans breaking out of multi-year bases. Crude steady near $102.50. None of these are headline trades. All of them point the same direction, and a new Fed governor is coming in soon, which adds translation risk to whatever Powell's framework was.

The Pearlman read in the closing segment pointed at how this regime gets blown. The instinctive mistake that traders make in down regimes is averaging down on losers. The mistake in this regime is the opposite. Investors are selling winners too soon, taking the dopamine hit of locking in gains, and then watching the same names grind higher without them. The cure offered today was unusually plain. Play smaller, because the inability to follow a plan is almost always a sizing problem. Get healthier, because stress tolerance is the actual underlying skill. Then sit on the winners.
