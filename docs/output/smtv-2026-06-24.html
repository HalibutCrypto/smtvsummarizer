Spencer called the whole crypto complex "basically unchanged" inside the first six minutes of the show and pulled up other tabs. $BTC around $65,200, $ETH around $1,770, $SOL around $74.60, total crypto market cap holding at $2.21 trillion. The texture matters more than the prices today. The macro backdrop was openly hostile to anything most allocators still treat as a risk currency. Gold dropped another -3%, silver another -5%, oil printed below $70 for the first time since February. The dollar index broke above 100 to 101. The textbook reaction in $BTC is to roll over with the metals. It didn't.[5:26]

The reason the desk shrugged is that there is genuinely less to do day-to-day in this corner of the market right now. Spencer reminisced about the cycle when JC was buying metaverse real estate, the desk was staking NFTs, yield farming, and running mining rigs at home. That ecosystem is gone. The take Strazza put on the screen was that there was a massive brain drain from crypto to AI, with the engineering talent that built the last cycle now shipping for OpenAI and Anthropic instead. Both companies have filed S-1s. Neither has a date. The desk expected the road shows to start within days of SpaceX going public last week and has been waiting on the announcement.[7:00]

The trade-level read is that $BTC ignoring a 101 dollar print is its own piece of information. Every other risk asset with no carry attached, gold and silver being the obvious cousins, is bleeding into the breakout. $BTC isn't. If the dollar rolls back over the way Strazza eventually expects, the metals reload and crypto wakes up. If the dollar keeps grinding higher and crypto still holds, the read is that the asset has decoupled from the dollar trade in a way it never had before.[8:00]

Micron reports tonight after the close and the expected move on options is roughly $170 give or take. Strazza's read on the pre-earnings selldown was straightforward. When a stock gets sold this aggressively just ahead of a print, the market is usually telling you it ran too far, too fast. The whole semiconductor space will move off the result. Chapter 3 · The Framework

Two Chinese Stock Markets and Only One Is Working

There are two ways to own China and they are diverging hard. The H-shares are Hong Kong-listed, where the tech and growth-y names live. $KWEB (KraneShares CSI China Internet ETF, the standard tech-heavy proxy) and $FXI (iShares China Large-Cap) sit inside ugly multi-year drawdowns. The A-shares are mainland Shanghai and Shenzhen, where industrials, financials and most of the actual domestic economy trade. $ASHR (Xtrackers Harvest CSI 300, the A-share proxy) is on what Strazza called "an absolute ripper," running from 27 to 37 over the last year. For an index that size, that is a real move.[22:10]

The driver is policy. The Chinese government has been running a steady push to get the domestic retail base into mainland-listed stocks. Not forced buying, just a heavy nudge that has shown up in flows. The H-share market is where the global capital that fled in 2021 never came back. The A-share market is where the policy carrot lives and where the daily marginal buyer actually sits. $KBA (the KraneShares Bosera MSCI China A 50 ETF) is the other A-share vehicle. Far less liquid than $ASHR, but it has been around forever and KraneShares were the original pioneers of US-listed China index products.[23:15]

The trap for US investors who tried to "buy China" over the last decade is that they were almost certainly buying H-shares. Spencer owned $KWEB ten years ago when the pitch was the demographic story, and admitted on air that it didn't work out. Strazza flagged India as the next demographic story now hitting retail with the same population-growth framing, and his read was to stay away while it is being marketed. The pattern is that by the time a population-driven narrative arrives at the broker app, the trade is usually late.[26:30]

For anyone holding emerging-markets exposure, two questions matter. Which index do you actually own. And how comfortable are you with a trade whose driver is policy rather than fundamentals. A policy-driven push is tradable while the policy is in place. It also reverses faster than a fundamental thesis would. The setup is real, the move has been real, and the vehicle selection is the entire trade.
