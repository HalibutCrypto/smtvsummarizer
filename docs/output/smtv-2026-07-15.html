The whole episode was built around one idea. Strazza put up chart after chart of enterprise software leaders and they all looked identical. Data Dog ($DDOG, an observability platform for cloud infrastructure), Hewlett Packard Enterprise ($HPE, the enterprise server and networking business Meg Whitman spun out of HP), NetApp ($NTAP, storage and data management for enterprise IT), Crowd Strike ($CRWD), Snowflake ($SNOW), and Octa ($OKTA). Each one showed a monster breakaway gap on the last earnings report, a five to six week high-and-tight consolidation on top of that gap, and now a curl higher on Strazza's proprietary volatility indicator that measures compression giving way to expansion. Same trade, six times.

Crowd Strike and Octa were the first two names in that group to fire yesterday. Crowd Strike ran twelve percent. Octa ran ten. Strazza said out loud that he had banned taking doubles on options positions in the group this week because he expects the rest of the basket to make the same move. The trades expire this Friday and the following one.

The frame that made the software basket interesting was the second point. Semiconductors and software have historically moved together, with a correlation coefficient (a statistical measure of how tightly two things track, running from negative one to positive one) that lives up in the mid-seventies. Right now that number is near zero. It has been near zero since last year, and the day-to-day directional split has only widened. Money is not leaving big tech. It is rotating from semis into software inside the same institutional accounts.

Palantir ($PLTR) got its own moment. Strazza's read was that the recent breakdown of the February-to-May range was a failed pattern that is now setting up a fast move higher. The weekly RSI never registered oversold, which he flagged as the tell that healthy uptrends do not. Volatility on the stock has compressed to the tightest level on record for the name. His book is long in the IRA and long options short-dated into next week, sized for a break out of the coil.

Bank earnings continue to print clean. Morgan Stanley reported trading revenue growth of fifty to sixty percent. JPMorgan and Goldman are growing top-line at roughly thirty percent. Wells Fargo is entering the options clearing market, a business it was locked out of for most of the last fifteen years under the growth cap. The desk kept returning to the same idea. Banks have quietly become an AI beneficiary trade in their own right, both because they finance the capex build-out and because they are internal adopters using the tools to cut cost.

Energy is the other side that lit up this morning. The 321 crack spread (a measure of the gross profit margin refiners earn turning crude oil into gasoline and heating oil) printed a new all-time high. So did the December heating oil contract. So did the oil refiners ETF ($CRAK). The straightforward mental model is that the refiners are apple pie stands, and if they are making record margins on pies, they are going to buy more apples, which is crude oil. Crude pushed $79.80 this morning off the ceasefire coming off the table again. Names like Phillips 66 ($PSX) and Valero ($VLO) are already at fresh highs.

One name to keep on the radar

PayPal ($PYPL) is fielding takeover headlines this morning. The desk's read is that a takeout in the sixties is on the table, which would leave a lot of retail bag holders who bought the stock during the fintech peak at two hundred with a very ugly exit. It is a reminder that the same "money-good, one-day-worth-owning" name can round-trip a decade of narrative before the strategic-buyer bid finally shows up.

Chapter 3 · The Framework

Hedge Funds Are the Most Short the NASDAQ in Three Years and the Primary Trend Just Hit 300 Days

Two facts, one behind the other, ran through the whole morning. Speculators (the CFTC futures data lumps hedge funds and asset managers together under that label) are net-short the NASDAQ 100 e-mini futures at the most extreme level since Spring 2023. The last time they were positioned this bearish, the NASDAQ was months away from a run that took it from 12,000 to 20,000 and beyond. Right before that run, the same crowd was as net-long as they had ever been, and they faded the entire October-November 2023 melt-up.

Sitting on top of that positioning read, Spencer quietly noted that the 200-day moving average of the S&P 500 has now been rising for 300 straight days in a row. That is a technical way of saying the primary trend of the biggest index in the world has been up, uninterrupted, for ten months. The desk has been trained by twenty years of bull markets to see this stat as unremarkable, which is exactly the point. It is not.

“These are speculators. So these are hedge funds, asset managers. These are the worst investors in the world consistently. This is bullish as hell. Look at how that positioning flipped right into the consolidation.”
JC, on the NASDAQ 100 speculator net-short print [16:50]

JC leaned on Morgan Housel's frame to close the loop. Everyone is a product of the market they came up in. Strazza started his career in 2010. Spencer graduated college in 2013. Both careers have been spent watching tech leadership work over and over again while the punditry told them it had to end. The other side of the same generational sample is Michael Burry, who came up in the 1998-2002 unwind and has been positioned for the top ever since. Both readings are honest. Only one has been paid.

The specific trade this framework points to is not more complicated than it looks. When the tape's primary trend has been up for 300 days and the biggest allocators are positioned the most short they have been in three years, the pain trade is up. Continuation patterns resolve in the direction of the trend more often than they reverse. That is the same argument the six software coils in Chapter 2 are making, only expressed in position data.
